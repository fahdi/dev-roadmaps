How do they agree a key in the open?

This is the part that sounds impossible. Two machines that have never met need a shared secret, while someone records the entire conversation.

They each generate a private value, send only a public half, and combine the other side's half with their own private one. Both arrive at the same result and the eavesdropper cannot derive it from the two public halves alone. Modern TLS also generates fresh values per session, so recording traffic today and stealing the server key later still does not decrypt it.

Encryption without identity is worthless

An encrypted channel only guarantees that whoever is on the other end can read it. If that is an attacker, you have encrypted your password directly to them.

The certificate is what ties the key to a name, checked against authorities your device already trusts. The padlock means private and verified, not safe, since a phishing domain can hold a perfectly valid certificate for its own name.

What encryption still leaks

The contents are unreadable. Plenty around them is not.

The observer still sees which server you connected to and how much you sent and when. Sizes and timing alone can identify a page. The domain is often visible during setup unless the newer encrypted handshake extension is in use, and DNS may have announced where you were going before the connection even opened.

Where TLS actually ends

People picture one tunnel from browser to application. In practice it usually terminates at the edge, at a CDN or load balancer.

From there it is re-encrypted, or occasionally not. Encryption in transit says nothing about the hops after termination, nothing about how it is stored, and nothing about a compromised endpoint. A key logger on the device wins regardless of the protocol.

They copy the same number of packets either way.
Encryption does not stop capture, it removes comprehension, and that distinction is the whole point.
An eavesdropper on plain HTTP is quietly assembling a list of credentials. On HTTPS they are assembling a pile of ciphertext, a record of who you talked to, and nothing they can use.
Roadmap: agree a key in the open, verify who is on the other end, encrypt the contents, then stay aware of the metadata and of where the tunnel stops.
